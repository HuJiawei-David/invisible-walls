The Invisible Walls

A Manifesto for Cross-Cultural Belonging at the University of Auckland

Step Through the Glass. Build Belonging Together.

We share lecture theatres, libraries and campus spaces, yet an invisible wall can still keep domestic and international students apart, so here are five practical ways to open a door, together.

Read the five demands Try Say One Hello

Context / Background

Why this matters

The University of Auckland is a highly diverse university community. In 2025, it enrolled 49,901 students, including 11,759 overseas students, meaning almost one in four students came from outside New Zealand (University of Auckland, 2026).

Yet diversity in numbers does not automatically create meaningful connection. Students from different cultural backgrounds may share lecture theatres, libraries and campus spaces while still remaining socially separated. Differences in language confidence, communication styles, cultural expectations and established friendship groups can create invisible barriers that affect who speaks to whom, who feels comfortable participating, and who feels that they belong.

This problem has been recognised in New Zealand research for decades. A Ministry of Education review concluded that having large numbers of international students is not enough to create intercultural interaction or friendship. Instead, contact needs to be deliberately structured through strategies such as peer-pairing and cooperative learning (Ward, 2001). A national survey later found that 70% of international students wanted more New Zealand friends, while around one in three identified language skills as a barrier to forming those friendships (Ward & Masgoret, 2004).

This is why our manifesto is not simply about increasing diversity. The University is already diverse. Our aim is to turn diversity into connection, participation and belonging.

An ethic of care

Narrative / Frame

Our manifesto challenges a common narrative: if students from different cultural backgrounds share the same campus, meaningful integration will happen naturally.

We reframe the issue through the idea that belonging must be built together. Cross-cultural integration should not be treated as something international students must achieve by learning how to “fit in”. Domestic students, international students, lecturers, student organisations and the University all share responsibility for creating opportunities for connection.

Our frame is based on an ethic of care. Rather than blaming students for remaining within familiar groups, we focus on making interaction easier, safer and more meaningful. This shifts the issue from individual adaptation to collective responsibility.

Values and Emotions

Our manifesto is built around the values of belonging, empathy, inclusion, community and interconnection. We want a university where students do not simply share the same physical space, but feel recognised and connected to people from different backgrounds.

The emotions we aim to activate are hope, empathy, solidarity and optimism. We deliberately avoid guilt, blame and fear. Instead, we want students to feel capable of creating change through realistic actions such as meeting someone new, joining a regular group, speaking in a lower-pressure environment, saying hello or sharing a story.

Belonging should feel possible, shared and worth building.

Five doors through the glass

The five demands

Each door is one practical action, with the people who can make it happen.

Demand 1

Mix Every Group

We call on course coordinators to form mixed domestic–international groups for at least one group assignment in every course, and to publish the group lists in the course outline.

Students often remain within familiar social and cultural circles. Structured group work creates opportunities to interact with people they might not otherwise approach. Research reviewed by the New Zealand Ministry of Education suggests that cooperative learning can increase intercultural contact and reduce negative stereotypes between domestic and international students (Ward, 2001). A mixed group may not automatically create friendship, but it can open the first door.

Demand 2

Meet Every Week

We call on AUSA and student programmes such as International Buddies and UniGuide to run small groups that meet weekly, with the same members, for the whole semester. Genuine connection needs repeated interaction, not just one-off events.

One orientation event can introduce people, but relationships need time to develop. Repeated interaction allows students to move beyond first impressions, become more comfortable with one another and build trust.

New Zealand research has also highlighted the value of peer-pairing and structured contact in supporting interaction between domestic and international students (Ward, 2001).

Connection needs continuity.

Who acts
AUSA and student programmes such as International Buddies and UniGuide

Demand 3

Lower the Pressure

We call on lecturers and tutors to build at least one low-pressure way to participate into every tutorial, such as pair discussions, written responses, or digital chat options. Language confidence should not decide who gets to speak and belong.

Participation should not depend on who speaks the fastest or feels most confident in English. A national survey found that around one-third of international students identified language skills as a barrier to making New Zealand friends (Ward & Masgoret, 2004).

Pair discussions, written responses and digital options give students more ways to contribute. A pause, an accent, or imperfect English should never make someone feel invisible.

Who acts
Lecturers and tutors

Demand 4

Say One Hello

We challenge every student to start at least one conversation each week with someone from a different cultural background, and to tick it off on our webpage counter. Small actions can make cross-cultural connection feel more normal, visible, and achievable.

Institutions can create opportunities, but students also shape campus culture through everyday choices. A national New Zealand survey found that 70% of international students wanted more New Zealand friends (Ward & Masgoret, 2004). The desire for connection already exists; sometimes what is missing is the first step.

You do not need to change the whole campus today. Start with one person.

Who acts
Every student

Demand 5

Tell It Your Way

We invite every student, especially international students, to share one story in the format they choose (text, photo, voice, video, or another language). We call on the University and AUSA to host these stories only with the storyteller’s consent, so they keep control over what is shared.

Statistics can describe diversity, but they cannot fully show what belonging, exclusion or connection feels like.

Personal stories allow students to describe these experiences in their own way. Giving students control over the format, language and content of their stories creates space for different voices rather than speaking for them.

Different voices. Different stories. One shared campus.

Who acts
Every student, especially international students; the University and AUSA

Final Call

Final Call

We do not want a university that is diverse only in statistics.

We want a university where diversity becomes conversation, understanding, participation, friendship and community.

The glass between us may be difficult to see, but it is not permanent. Every mixed group, weekly meeting, safer conversation, hello and shared story creates another way through.
